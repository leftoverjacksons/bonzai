Why 2.5D suits ink wash

Ink painters show distance with dilution. Near forms get dense black ink; far forms get pale, watery grey. The Japanese term is 濃淡 (nōtan), dark and light. A 2.5D renderer already knows each stroke's depth, so it can choose the ink density from depth. Turning the tree is then the same as repainting it from another side: a branch swinging behind the trunk fades to grey, and one swinging toward you darkens.

The thick strokes also show 飛白 (hihaku, "flying white"), the pale streaks a dry brush leaves. In 3D, effects like these must be faked on lit surfaces. In 2.5D they are simply how each stroke is painted.

What 2.5D gives up

The camera cannot tilt to look down on the tree from above. Overlap is decided by sorting strokes by depth, so two objects that pass through each other, such as a branch and a pillar, can briefly draw in the wrong order. Lighting is not physical. None of these matter much for a camera that orbits a vertical tree, and the sorting errors can be reduced by splitting long objects into short pieces.

Examples to look at

  • Nebulus / Tower Toppler (1987)A 2D-drawn tower that rotates as you climb it. The closest precedent for climbing a turning vertical structure.
  • Prune (2015)A mobile game about growing and pruning a tree toward light while avoiding hazards. Flat 2D and minimal. It is the nearest existing design, so Bonzai should differ from it on purpose: rotation, the card hand, and the ruins.
  • Shan Shui (Lingdong Huang)An endless Chinese ink landscape generated in the browser. A reference for procedural ink brushwork in the backdrop.
  • Ōkami (2006)Full 3D with a sumi-e shader. It shows the 3D route can look painterly, at the cost of a studio's shader work.
  • Fez (2012)A 2D world that rotates in 90° steps around the vertical axis. Another kind of 2.5D.
  • Alto's Odyssey (2018)Layered 2D scenery on phones. A reference for calm mood, mist, and atmospheric depth.
